I identify its ends and compare a point’s position from each side. When both readings use the same span and match, the point can only be halfway between them.</p><p>Now I give that construction a family of numbers. I remove zero, one and every composite from the candidate list. What remains is exactly the primes. Each prime retains its identity while its admitted matching event occupies the midpoint of its parent span. Sharing a location does not make them the same number.</p><p>I call this Euler-Zeno in Prime Relatives (EZPR). Infinite addressing describes the entire family through one rule and one argument, without plotting every member separately. The proofs establish midpoint placement under matching, preservation through changes of scale and successive generations, and preservation of inherited distinctions through exact recovery. Lean verifies the arithmetic selection and matching-based placement results.</p><p>The principal result is proved local closure of EZPR: every admitted prime event has its unique parent-relative coordinate ½, and no admitted prime event lies off that crease. Matching is a defining rule of EZPR. Introduction</h2><p>Riemann’s 1859 paper connects prime counting to the zeros of the zeta function, beginning with Euler’s product over primes [<a href="#ref-6">6</a>]. Bombieri’s exposition explains the hypothesis and why those zeros matter for the distribution of primes [<a href="#ref-7">7</a>]. That connection motivates this investigation. The results below concern a separately defined coordinate model; the shared appearance of one-half does not identify its events with zeta zeros.</p><p>I named this construction Euler-Zeno in Prime Relatives (EZPR): a nod to Euler’s primes and Zeno’s half-steps. The construction and the claims below are mine.</p><p>The value is the same. Its address is different.</p><p>That distinction is where this construction starts. I can move a reference without changing its value. I can also give two different numbers the same location without making them the same number. To keep those statements straight, I need the number, its address and the frame that makes the address meaningful.</p><p>First, remove zero and one from the candidate family. Then remove the composites. Nine leaves because three times three is nine. Seven stays. The surviving labels are the primes. A number can leave the active comparison while its record stays available.</p><p>Now the placement question: where does a surviving number&#x27;s comparison occur? I give the event an identified parent segment and read its position from both ends. The rule is that the two relative readings match. Equal progress from opposite ends of the same span puts the event halfway between them. The proof is short. Keeping track of what the proof actually uses takes more care.</p><p>Selection tells me which numbers remain. Matching tells me where their events sit. The first does not silently supply the second. In this paper, matching is an explicit rule of the construction.</p><p>Two, three and seven can therefore share a midpoint and still be two, three and seven. A coordinate is not an identity shredder. Their labels and histories stay with their records. If their parent spans differ, their actual locations can differ too: halfway along one span is not necessarily the same address as halfway along another.</p><p>This is where I use infinite addressing. I define the whole family, take an arbitrary member and prove the relation for it. The argument then covers every member. I do not need to plot each one to state that result. That is a universal argument, not a claim that a machine has finished an infinite run.</p><p>The research began with the Riemann hypothesis: every nontrivial zeta zero has real part one-half [<a href="#ref-4">4</a>, §25.10]. Here I establish the coordinate construction first. Section J identifies the further connection needed to carry that result back to the original question.</p><p>The order matters: identify the record, select the prime, place its matching event, then follow it through a change of frame and another generation. At the end, recover what was carried. Each step has to leave the next one something it can actually use. The companion README gives the exact Lean coverage and reproduction details.</p><h2 id="section-1-1-delivery-mechanics-and-relative-addresses" class="subsection">A.1 Delivery mechanics and relative addresses</h2><p>Follow the same participant through a change of address. Its identity stays with the record, and earlier records remain available for comparison. A new address does not require a new identity.</p><p>Definition — The continuing witness and its relative address.</p><p>A witness here is the identified participant being tracked. Let W denote that identity, n a nonnegative stage number, and A<sub>n</sub> its address on the chosen real coordinate line. For any position x on that line, define</p><div class="equation" id="EQ01"><span>r<sub>n</sub>(x) = x − A<sub>n</sub></span><small>EQ01</small></div><p>Consequently,</p><div class="equation" id="EQ02"><span>r<sub>n</sub>(A<sub>n</sub>) = 0</span><small>EQ02</small></div><p>This is a translation of the coordinate origin [<a href="#ref-2">2</a>, Chapter 2]: the witness is at zero relative to its own address. The zero is a reference reading, not a claim that the witness has disappeared or that a zeta function vanishes.</p><p>For example, at address A<sub>0</sub>=3 the reading is r<sub>0</sub>(x)=x−3. At address A<sub>1</sub>=8 it is r<sub>1</sub>(x)=x−8. The witness reads zero at each of those addresses. The addresses differ; the tracked identity W does not. These chosen addresses illustrate the definition; the formula does not generate the next address.</p><p class="figure-introduction">Figure 1 compares two readings of the same locations: the parent address and the address relative to one-half. Changing the reference does not move the point.</p><figure id="figure-1"><div class="num">FIGURE 1</div><h2 id="figure-1-title">Relative coordinates in half-unit steps</h2><svg id="figure-1-diagram" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" viewBox="0 0 720 170" preserveAspectRatio="xMinYMid meet" role="img"><g font-family="sans-serif" fill="#182c34" font-size="17"><text x="10" y="37">Parent x</text><text x="10" y="120">r(x) = x − ½</text><path d="M185 35H665M185 118H665" stroke="#176a65" stroke-width="3"/></path><path d="M305 14V140" stroke="#ae4d27" stroke-dasharray="4 4"/></path><path d="M185 30v10M185 113v10" stroke="#182c34"/></path><text x="185" y="60" text-anchor="middle">0</text><text x="185" y="147" text-anchor="middle">−½</text><path d="M305 30v10M305 113v10" stroke="#182c34"/></path><text x="305" y="60" text-anchor="middle">½</text><text x="305" y="147" text-anchor="middle">0</text><path d="M425 30v10M425 113v10" stroke="#182c34"/></path><text x="425" y="60" text-anchor="middle">1</text><text x="425" y="147" text-anchor="middle">½</text><path d="M545 30v10M545 113v10" stroke="#182c34"/></path><text x="545" y="60" text-anchor="middle">1½</text><text x="545" y="147" text-anchor="middle">1</text><path d="M665 30v10M665 113v10" stroke="#182c34"/></path><text x="665" y="60" text-anchor="middle">2</text><text x="665" y="147" text-anchor="middle">1½</text><circle cx="305" cy="35" r="6" fill="#ae4d27"/></circle><circle cx="305" cy="118" r="6" fill="#ae4d27"/></circle><text x="430" y="89" text-anchor="middle">Reference A = ½ · same point reads 0 locally</text></g></svg><div class="key" id="figure-1-key">A: reference address. r(x): relative reading. For clarity, write that record sequence as</p><div class="equation" id="EQ03"><span>H<sub>n</sub> = ((W, A<sub>0</sub>), (W, A<sub>1</sub>), ..., (W, A<sub>n</sub>))</span><small>EQ03</small></div><p class="retention-paragraph">The stack keeps the identity and each recorded address together. Reading zero locally leaves those earlier addresses intact. This is the information represented by the example; the notation specifies no storage mechanism or additional inherited relationships.</p><p>Keeping a stack is useful only if I can get the required information back. That is the recovery question. Theorem 6 states the test precisely: recover after carrying, and obtain the inherited state. An implementation must satisfy that test to claim exact recovery.</p><h2 id="section-2-definitions-and-retained-state">B. Definitions and retained state</h2><p>Set operations and indexed families use standard notation [<a href="#ref-3">3</a>, §§1.5, 1.8]. The records and admission rule below are definitions of the present model.</p><p>The definitions below separate the number being classified, the record that identifies it, and the position assigned to an admitted comparison event.</p><h2 id="section-2-1-candidates-and-cancellation" class="subsection">B.1 Candidates and cancellation</h2><p>Let N<sub>0</sub> denote the nonnegative integers:</p><div class="equation" id="natural-numbers"><span>N<sub>0</sub> = {0, 1, 2, …}</span><small></small></div><p>A proper factorization means n = u × v, where u and v are integers at least two. Define the composite set C and the surviving family S together:</p><div class="equation" id="EQ04"><span><span id="composite-set">C = {u × v : u, v ∈ N<sub>0</sub>, u ≥ 2, v ≥ 2}</span><br>S = N<sub>0</sub> ∖ ({0, 1} ∪ C)</span><small>EQ04</small></div><p>Thus S is the surviving candidate family. <a href="#theorem-1">Theorem 1</a> proves that S is exactly the primes; this equivalence is not needed as an assumption in its definition.</p><p>Cancellation excludes a candidate from this active family. It need not erase its retained record. Excluding the numerical candidates zero and one also does not remove zero and unit reference roles from the coordinate construction.</p><h2 id="section-2-2-records-parents-and-positions" class="subsection">B.2 Records, parents and positions</h2><p>A label identifies a number. A record keeps that label together with the information required to interpret its event. For each p∈S, the proposed placement model supplies a record containing p, participant identities, parent identity K<sub>p</sub>, endpoints a<sub>p</sub>&lt;b<sub>p</sub>, event location x<sub>p</sub> and relevant history.</p><p>The parent span is the segment between those endpoints. Its length is L<sub>p</sub>=b<sub>p</sub>−a<sub>p</sub>&gt;0. All event locations belong to the declared affine X line. There is no independent Y component in this model&#x27;s location type; this is a model definition, not a reduction theorem for another system.</p><p>Distinct labels may share a location. Equal endpoint values do not by themselves identify participants or their histories.</p><h2 id="section-2-3-relative-readings-incidence-and-matching" class="subsection">B.3 Relative readings, incidence and matching</h2><p>For the same event and identified parent, define</p><div class="equation" id="EQ05"><span>t<sub>p</sub> = (x<sub>p</sub> − a<sub>p</sub>)/L<sub>p</sub>, u<sub>p</sub> = (b<sub>p</sub> − x<sub>p</sub>)/L<sub>p</sub></span><small>EQ05</small></div><p>These are directed parent-relative coordinates. For an event inside the parent segment, they are the fractions of the span traversed from its two endpoints. The positive length makes both expressions well-defined. If participants begin in different charts, a supplied relative-coordinate map must first identify the common event and parent being compared.</p><p>Incidence is the identity t<sub>p</sub>+u<sub>p</sub>=1, obtained directly from these definitions. Matching is the additional admission rule t<sub>p</sub>=u<sub>p</sub>. A matching record satisfies both equations and the location relation x<sub>p</sub>=a<sub>p</sub>+L<sub>p</sub> t<sub>p</sub>. <a href="#theorem-2">Theorem 2</a> derives its unique midpoint location.</p><p>The definitions do not derive matching from primality, from available perspectives, or from the equality of reference labels. The whole-family theorem explicitly requires a matching record for each survivor.</p><h2 id="section-2-4-retention-and-terminology" class="subsection">B.4 Retention and terminology</h2><p>Here “crease” means the identified parent midpoint. “Projector” names a participant role in the source construction; the affine equations below represent a comparison and do not independently establish native projector behavior. “Closure” means that the declared conditions imply the stated conclusions throughout the admitted family.</p><p>Retention means keeping enough information to recover the specified inherited state. <a href="#theorem-6">Theorem 6</a> states the exact recovery condition; storing a history label alone is not that condition.</p><p>The revised terminology distinguishes perspective, connecting relation, comparison and reception. Perspective alone does not imply a receiver, reflection, doubled scale or new coordinate axis. Value, Perception and Action describe respectively the retained state, its admitted comparison and the committed operation; this usage introduces no physical timing schedule.</p><h2 class="theorem-chapter" id="section-3-arithmetic-selection">C. Prime Survivor Equivalence Theorem</h2><p>Start with the whole numbers. Remove zero and one, then every number made by multiplying two whole numbers greater than one. Nine goes because three times three is nine; seven and two stay. Every prime survives, and every survivor is prime. The proof below establishes both directions.</p><div class="proof-unit"><div class="statement-block"><h3 id="theorem-1">Statement and proof</h3><p>We use the standard definition of prime and composite integers [<a href="#ref-1">1</a>, Definition 1.1.3].</p><p>For n∈N<sub>0</sub>, n∈S if and only if n is prime.</p><div class="equation theorem-formula" id="theorem-1-formula"><span>n ∈ S ⇔ n is prime</span></div></div><p>Proof. Membership supplies n≥2 and excludes every factorization n=uv with both factors at least two. Thus n has no proper divisor d with 2≤d&lt;n: such a divisor would give n=dv and v≥2. Conversely, if n is prime, n≥2 and any such factorization would provide a proper divisor u, because v≥2 forces u&lt;n. Hence no proper factorization exists and n∈S. □</p></div><p>The statement classifies the whole family by the standard prime/composite distinction [<a href="#ref-1">1</a>, §1.1.1]. It supplies no midpoint-placement theorem.</p><p>This selection classifies labels. It does not say that a geometric point labeled 9 cannot be placed at a midpoint.</p><p class="figure-introduction">Figure 2 separates the surviving prime labels from the excluded labels. <p>The construction has three jobs: select the numbers, place their events and preserve the records. Each theorem settles one part of that work.</p>
<p><strong>Selection.</strong> Removing zero, one and proper products leaves exactly the primes (Section C).</p>
<p><strong>Placement.</strong> When two readings of the same event use the same parent span and satisfy the matching rule, the event is uniquely at that span’s midpoint (Section D). Applying this result to each supplied matching record gives local prime-family closure (Section E). Selection alone does not supply matching.</p>
<p><strong>Preservation.</strong> A common affine change preserves midpoint placement (Section F). Valid initial records and a validity-preserving recursion preserve it at every finite stage (Section G). Exact recovery makes the carry map injective on inherited states (Section H).</p>
<p>Together, these results close the declared EZPR model. Section J states what remains to connect that closure to the Riemann hypothesis. Formal verification and reproduction details are in the companion README.</p><h2 id="section-9-discussion-and-conclusion">J. Discussion and conclusion</h2><p>Two comparisons place this result in the wider literature. Lagarias proves that a sequence of inequalities involving harmonic numbers and sums of divisors is equivalent to the Riemann hypothesis [<a href="#ref-9">9</a>, Theorem 1.1]. This demonstrates that a formulation using integers can address the hypothesis, provided the equivalence itself is proved. EZPR’s classification and midpoint theorems do not yet supply such an equivalence.</p><p>Platt and Trudgian rigorously verify the location of zeta zeros up to height 3 × 10<sup>12</sup>, using interval arithmetic [<a href="#ref-10">10</a>]. Their result concerns a bounded region of the zeta function. Here, universal quantification covers every admitted EZPR record, but the objects quantified over are model records. A large verified region, an entire defined family, and an equivalence with the zeta-zero problem are distinct mathematical achievements.</p><p>What did the construction deliver? Cancellation leaves exactly the primes. Matching puts each admitted event halfway along its own parent. When records share a parent, they share that location. Their identities stay with them.</p><p>Now move it. Shift the whole parent or change its scale, and the event stays halfway. Carry the records through another generation under the preservation rules, and the match survives. Exact recovery answers the last question: can I get the inherited information back? If I can, two different inherited states cannot have been merged along the way.</p><p>That is local closure in EZPR. I supply equal-progress matching as a construction rule; the midpoint follows from it, incidence and location. Primality determines the family. Matching determines placement. The companion README traces the relationship to the earlier research constructions.</p><p>Two connections remain outside these proofs. The native constructor must supply the declared matching records. To settle the Riemann hypothesis, I must then connect the model to every nontrivial zeta zero and preserve its real coordinate. Neither connection is established here. A shared one-half is a reason to investigate, not the missing correspondence.</p><p>Return to the opening line. The value is the same. Its address is different. We can now say exactly what survives that change: identity, matched placement under the stated rules, and inherited information when recovery is exact. The record has moved. We can still account for what arrived.</p><h2 id="section-10-methodological-note">K. Methodological note</h2><p>I supplied the construction, terminology corrections and research direction. Analytic continuation extends it to other complex inputs, except for a pole at s = 1. A zero is an input where ζ(s) = 0. The zeros at −2, −4, −6, … are called trivial zeros. The Riemann hypothesis asserts that every other zero has real part exactly ½ [<a href="#ref-4">4</a>], [<a href="#ref-7">7</a>].</p><p>Write an input as s = σ + it. This is a statement about zeros of a particular function, not about all numbers.
