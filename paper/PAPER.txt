Abstract
I draw a line. I identify its ends and compare a point’s position from each side. When both 
readings use the same span and match, the point can only be halfway between them.
Now I give that construction a family of numbers. I remove zero, one and every composite from 
the candidate list. What remains is exactly the primes. Each prime retains its identity while its 
admitted matching event occupies the midpoint of its parent span. Sharing a location does not 
make them the same number.
I call this Euler-Zeno in Prime Relatives (EZPR). Infinite addressing describes the entire family 
through one rule and one argument, without plotting every member separately. The proofs 
establish midpoint placement under matching, preservation through changes of scale and 
successive generations, and preservation of inherited distinctions through exact recovery. Lean
verifies the arithmetic selection and matching-based placement results.
The principal result is proved local closure of EZPR: every admitted prime event has its unique 
parent-relative coordinate ½, and no admitted prime event lies off that crease. Matching is a 
defining rule of EZPR. The Riemann-hypothesis objective additionally requires a correspondence 
covering every nontrivial zeta zero and preserving its real part; that correspondence is not 
established in the archived proof.
I give an exact readout of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function on its critical line, preserving 
positions and multiplicities. The continued logarithmic derivative recovers the full nontrivial zero
family as poles at their original positions. Under the reviewed rational enclosure bounds, exactly 
one simple zero lies in a specified rectangle; reflection symmetry fixes it on the critical line. The
included certificate and verifier passed a 128-disk replay on 7 November 2026 in the retained 
implementation. For every original nontrivial zero ρ, full recovery retains (Y, d), where Y = Im ρ 
and d = Re ρ − ½. Recovery from Y alone holds if and only if d = 0 for every such zero. The
established closure concerns admitted prime events and the signed critical-line readout.

A. Introduction
Riemann’s 1859 paper connects prime counting to the zeros of the zeta function, beginning with 
Euler’s product over primes [6]. Bombieri’s exposition explains the hypothesis and why those 
zeros matter for the distribution of primes [7]. That connection motivates this investigation. The
results below concern a separately defined coordinate model; the shared appearance of one-half 
does not identify its events with zeta zeros.
I named this construction Euler-Zeno in Prime Relatives (EZPR): a nod to Euler’s primes and 
Zeno’s half-steps. The construction and the claims below are mine.
The value is the same. Its address is different.
That distinction is where this construction starts. I can move a reference without changing its 
value. I can also give two different numbers the same location without making them the same 
number. To keep those statements straight, I need the number, its address and the frame that 
makes the address meaningful.
First, remove zero and one from the candidate family. Then remove the composites. Nine leaves 
because three times three is nine. Seven stays. The surviving labels are the primes. A number can 
leave the active comparison while its record stays available.
Now the placement question: where does a surviving number's comparison occur? I give the 
event an identified parent segment and read its position from both ends. The rule is that the two 
relative readings match. Equal progress from opposite ends of the same span puts the event 
halfway between them. The proof is short. Keeping track of what the proof actually uses takes 
more care.
Selection tells me which numbers remain. Matching tells me where their events sit. The first does 
not silently supply the second. In this paper, matching is an explicit rule of the construction.
Two, three and seven can therefore share a midpoint and still be two, three and seven. A 
coordinate is not an identity shredder. Their labels and histories stay with their records. If their 
parent spans differ, their actual locations can differ too: halfway along one span is not necessarily
the same address as halfway along another.
This is where I use infinite addressing. I define the whole family, take an arbitrary member and 
prove the relation for it. The argument then covers every member. I do not need to plot each one 
to state that result. That is a universal argument, not a claim that a machine has finished an 
infinite run.
The research began with the Riemann hypothesis, which states that every nontrivial zeta zero has
real part one-half [4, §25.10]. Sections C–H establish the coordinate construction. Sections I–L 
then use the common frame to derive the critical-line readout, recover original zero positions 
through the prime-power transform, and certify placement in one rectangle.
The order matters: identify the record, select the prime, place its matching event, then follow it 
through a change of frame and another generation. At the end, recover what was carried. Each

a.1 Delivery mechanics and relative addresses
Follow the same participant through a change of address. Its identity stays with the record, and 
earlier records remain available for comparison. A new address does not require a new identity.
Definition — The continuing witness and its relative address.
A witness here is the identified participant being tracked. Let W denote that identity, n a 
nonnegative stage number, and An its address on the chosen real coordinate line. For any position
x on that line, define
rn(x) = x − An EQ01
Consequently,
rn(An) = 0 EQ02
This is a translation of the coordinate origin [2, Chapter 2]: the witness is at zero relative to its 
own address. The zero is a reference reading, not a claim that the witness has disappeared or that 
a zeta function vanishes.
For example, at address A0=3 the reading is r0(x)=x−3. At address A1=8 it is r1(x)=x−8. The witness 
reads zero at each of those addresses. The addresses differ; the tracked identity W does not. These 
chosen addresses illustrate the definition; the formula does not generate the next address.

B. Definitions and retained state
Set operations and indexed families use standard notation [3, §§1.5, 1.8]. The records and 
admission rule below are definitions of the present model.
The definitions below separate the number being classified, the record that identifies it, and the 
position assigned to an admitted comparison event.
b.1 Candidates and cancellation
Let N0 denote the nonnegative integers:
N0 = {0, 1, 2, …}
A proper factorization means n = u × v, where u and v are integers at least two. Define the 
composite set C and the surviving family S together:
C = {u × v : u, v ∈ N0, u ≥ 2, v ≥ 2}
S = N0 ∖ ({0, 1} ∪ C) EQ04
Thus S is the surviving candidate family. Theorem 1 proves that S is exactly the primes; this 
equivalence is not needed as an assumption in its definition.
Cancellation excludes a candidate from this active family. It need not erase its retained record. 
Excluding the numerical candidates zero and one also does not remove zero and unit reference 
roles from the coordinate construction.
b.2 Records, parents and positions
A label identifies a number. A record keeps that label together with the information required to 
interpret its event. For each p∈S, the proposed placement model supplies a record containing p, 
participant identities, parent identity Kp, endpoints ap<bp, event location xp and relevant history.

The parent span is the segment between those endpoints. Its length is Lp=bp−ap>0. All event
locations belong to the declared affine X line. There is no independent Y component in this

Distinct labels may share a location. Equal endpoint values do not by themselves identify

b.3 Relative readings, incidence and matching
For the same event and identified parent, define
tp = (xp − ap)/Lp, up = (bp − xp)/Lp EQ05
These are directed parent-relative coordinates. For an event inside the parent segment, they are 
the fractions of the span traversed from its two endpoints. The positive length makes both 
expressions well-defined. If participants begin in different charts, a supplied relative-coordinate 
map must first identify the common event and parent being compared.
Incidence is the identity tp+up=1, obtained directly from these definitions. Matching is the 
additional admission rule tp=up. A matching record satisfies both equations and the location 
relation xp=ap+Lp tp. Theorem 2 derives its unique midpoint location.
The definitions do not derive matching from primality, from available perspectives, or from the 
equality of reference labels. The whole-family theorem explicitly requires a matching record for 
each survivor.
b.4 Retention and terminology
Here “crease” means the identified parent midpoint. “Projector” names a participant role in the 
source construction; the affine equations below represent a comparison and do not independently
establish native projector behavior. “Closure” means that the declared conditions imply the stated
conclusions throughout the admitted family.
Retention means keeping enough information to recover the specified inherited state. Theorem 6
states the exact recovery condition; storing a history label alone is not that condition.
The revised terminology distinguishes perspective, connecting relation, comparison and 
reception. Perspective alone does not imply a receiver, reflection, doubled scale or new coordinate
axis. Value, Perception and Action describe respectively the retained state, its admitted 
comparison and the committed operation; this usage introduces no physical timing schedule.

two whole numbers greater than one. Nine goes because three times three is nine; seven and two 
stay. Every prime survives, and every survivor is prime. The proof below establishes both 
directions.

Statement and proof
We use the standard definition of prime and composite integers [1, Definition 1.1.3].
For n∈N0, n∈S if and only if n is prime.
n ∈ S ⇔ n is prime
Proof. Membership supplies n≥2 and excludes every factorization n=uv with both factors at least

E. Local Prime-Family Closure Theorem
Combine the first two results: select the prime labels, then equip each with a matching record. 
Each event lies at its own parent midpoint. Several distinct primes can share a location without 
losing their identities. Different parent spans need not have the same midpoint.
Statement and proof
Suppose every p∈S is equipped with real endpoints ap<bp and real readings tp,up,xp satisfying 
tp+up=1, tp=up and xp=ap+(bp−ap)tp. Every prime record then lies at its identified parent's midpoint. 
No such record lies strictly to either side of that midpoint.
∀p ∈ S: xp = (ap + bp)/2
Proof. Theorem 1 identifies S with the primes. Apply Theorem 2 to an arbitrary member. Either 
strict inequality contradicts the midpoint equality. Because the member was arbitrary, the 
conclusion holds universally without enumeration. □
Corollary — Shared parent, common X.
If all records have the same endpoints a,b, all matched locations equal (a+b)/2. More generally, 
equal parent midpoints suffice. Equal normalized progress alone is insufficient when the 
midpoints differ.

F. Affine Preservation Theorem
Affine maps preserve affine combinations [2, §2.7, Definition 2.6]. The midpoint case uses two 
weights, each equal to one-half; the proof below specializes that standard fact.
Shift or uniformly rescale both endpoints and their event together. On a segment from zero to 
ten, the midpoint is five. Double all three coordinates and add three: the endpoints become three 
and twenty-three, and the midpoint becomes thirteen. The same relationship survives. A zero 
scale is excluded because it collapses the span; a negative scale reverses orientation.
Statement and proof
For f(x)=αx+β with α≠0, midpoint membership is preserved when the complete event and parent
are transported together.
x = (a + b)/2 ⇒ f(x) = (f(a) + f(b))/2
Proof. If x=(a+b)/2, then f(x)=(f(a)+f(b))/2 by distribution. Also f(b)−f(a)=α(b−a)≠0, so the 
normalized comparison remains defined. Set a′=min(f(a),f(b)), b′=max(f(a),f(b)), and L′=b′−a′=|α|
L>0. Use t′=(f(x)−a′)/L′ and u′=(b′−f(x))/L′. When α<0 the endpoint order reverses, so t′=u and 
u′=t; when α>0, t′=t and u′=u. Thus matching is preserved in either case. Participant identities 
remain in their records independently of endpoint order. □
G. Recursive Preservation Theorem
The proof uses ordinary mathematical induction [3, §10.1] and the definition of an indexed union 
[3, §1.8].
Start with matching records. At each next stage, preserve the inherited matches and require every
new record to match. Every resulting stage then satisfies the rule. Any record in the union 
entered at some finite stage, so it satisfies the rule too. This does not establish that every prime
eventually enters.

Proof. The base is given. At the successor stage, inherited events match by preservation and new

H. Recoverable Carry is Injective Theorem
The argument uses function composition and injectivity [3, §§12.2, 12.4–12.5]. Exact recovery is 
the left-inverse condition; the proof below derives injectivity directly.
If carrying could turn two different inherited states into the same encoded state, one recovery 
operation would have to return two different originals from that same input. Exact recovery rules
this out: identical encodings must have identical originals. New experience may still be added 
alongside the inherited component.
Statement and proof
Let Hset be the admitted inherited states and Bset the encoded states. If functions K:Hset→Bset and 
R:K(Hset)→Hset satisfy R(K(H))=H for every H∈Hset, then K is injective.
R ∘ K = id ⇒ K is injective
Proof. K(H)=K(J) implies H=R(K(H))=R(K(J))=J. □
An enlarged successor can contain additional experience. The identity applies to its recoverable 
inherited component, not to equality of the entire enlarged state. Historical admission and 
current read validity also remain distinct.

K. Prime-power transform and pole recovery
Theorem 9 (Original zero recovery). In the open critical strip, the poles of the continued 
aggregate prime-power transform are precisely the nontrivial zeta zeros. Each pole is simple and 
its residue equals minus the zero’s multiplicity.
Proof. For ℜ s>1, logarithmic differentiation of the absolutely convergent Euler product gives
The quotient supplies meromorphic continuation. Let ρ be an arbitrary zero in the open critical 
strip. Zeta is holomorphic there and is not identically zero: its continuation is analytic on the 
connected domain ℂ ∖ {1} and ζ(2) ≠ 0. The identity theorem therefore makes ρ an isolated zero of
finite order m ≥ 1. The local order-of-vanishing theorem gives ζ(s) = (s − ρ)ᵐ g(s), with g 
holomorphic and g(ρ) ≠ 0. Shrink the neighborhood so g stays nonzero. Differentiation yields D(s)
= −m/(s − ρ) − g′(s)/g(s). The second term is holomorphic. At every nonzero point in the strip, the 
quotient is holomorphic. Thus the poles recover precisely the original zeros, and their negative 
residues recover multiplicities. This argument applies to arbitrary strip zeros, independently of 
their real coordinates. [4, 17]

The construction has three jobs: select the numbers, place their events and preserve the records.

N. Discussion and conclusion
Two comparisons place this result in the wider literature. Lagarias proves that a sequence of 
inequalities involving harmonic numbers and sums of divisors is equivalent to the Riemann 
hypothesis [9, Theorem 1.1]. This demonstrates that a formulation using integers can address the 
hypothesis, provided the equivalence itself is proved. EZPR’s classification and midpoint
theorems do not yet supply such an equivalence.
Platt and Trudgian rigorously verify the location of zeta zeros up to height 3 × 1012, using interval
arithmetic [10]. Their result concerns a bounded region of the zeta function. Here, universal 
quantification covers every admitted EZPR record, but the objects quantified over are model 
records. A large verified region, an entire defined family, and an equivalence with the zeta-zero 
problem are distinct mathematical achievements.
What did the construction deliver? Cancellation leaves exactly the primes. Matching puts each 
admitted event halfway along its own parent. When records share a parent, they share that 
location. Their identities stay with them.
Now move it. Shift the whole parent or change its scale, and the event stays halfway. Carry the 
records through another generation under the preservation rules, and the match survives. Exact 
recovery answers the last question: can I get the inherited information back? If I can, two 
different inherited states cannot have been merged along the way.
That is local closure in EZPR. I supply equal-progress matching as a construction rule; the 
midpoint follows from it, incidence and location. Primality determines the family. Matching 
determines placement. The companion README traces the relationship to the earlier research 
constructions.
The native constructor must still supply the declared matching records. For the zeta zeros, Section
K gives a full address consisting of the height and the real offset. Recovering every original zero 
from the height alone is equivalent to proving that each offset vanishes. Section L establishes this
placement for the zero inside the certified rectangle.
Return to the opening line. The value is the same. Its address is different. We can now say exactly 
what survives that change: identity, matched placement under the stated rules, and inherited 
information when recovery is exact. The record has moved. We can still account for what arrived.
